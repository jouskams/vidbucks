In 1885, a German psychologist named Hermann Ebbinghaus decided to test his own memory.

He memorized lists of meaningless syllables, then checked how many he could still recall later.

He repeated this again and again, for years, with nobody to help him.

What he found became famous. We call it the forgetting curve.

Right after learning, you remember a lot. Then memory drops quickly, and later the loss slows down.

Most of the loss happens early, in the first hours and days.

So if you never go back to what you learned, the curve simply takes it.

Here is the good news. Every time you bring a memory back, the curve gets flatter.

Think of a path in a forest. Walk it once, and the grass grows back. Walk it every week, and it becomes a road.

That is the secret behind everything that follows. Let's start with the biggest trap of all.

Fix number one. Stop rereading your notes.

Rereading feels productive. The words look familiar, and familiar feels like knowing.

Psychologists call this the illusion of knowing. You recognize the page, but you could not produce the answer.

Highlighting has the same problem. A large review of study techniques rated both of them as low in usefulness.

That does not mean they are useless. It means they are a weak use of your time.

A better question to ask yourself is not, do I recognize this? It is, can I produce this?

Sam's problem was never effort. Sam worked hard, but on the wrong kind of work.

Fix number two. Test yourself before you feel ready.

Close the book. Take a blank page. Write down everything you can remember.

Then open the book, and check what you missed.

This is called retrieval practice, and it is one of the best studied effects in learning research.

In classic experiments, students who practiced recalling material often remembered more a week later than students who simply reread it.

Even a wrong guess helps, because the right answer sticks better afterwards.

Flashcards work for exactly this reason, but only if you answer before you flip the card.

If it feels a little hard, good. That feeling is the learning.

Sam tries it. On the first attempt, Sam remembers four things out of twenty. That feels terrible, but now Sam knows exactly what to study.

Fix number three. Spread it out.

Studying the same topic for five hours in one night feels intense. But it mostly builds short term memory.

Cramming can get you through tomorrow's test. It rarely survives until next month.

Spaced repetition means you review something just as you are about to forget it.

For example, review it the next day, then three days later, then a week later, then a month later.

Each review is a retrieval, so each one flattens the forgetting curve a little more.

Several short sessions usually beat one long session, even when the total time is the same.

And they fit into a busy week much more easily than one giant night.

Apps like Anki can schedule the reviews for you. But a simple calendar works too.

Fix number four. Mix it up.

Most people practice one type of problem until it feels easy, and then move on. That is called blocked practice.

Interleaving means mixing different types of problems in the same session.

It feels worse. You make more mistakes while you practice.

But later, on a real test, mixed practice usually wins, because you learned to choose the right method, not just repeat one.

Doing math? Mix different problem types. Learning a language? Mix vocabulary, listening, and speaking.

Research on motor skills points the same way, from basketball drills to piano scales.

Fix number five. Sleep is part of studying.

While you sleep, your brain replays and stabilizes what you learned during the day.

People who sleep after learning usually remember more than people who stay awake for the same stretch of time.

So the all nighter is a trap. You give up the very thing that locks memories in, for a few extra hours.

A better plan is to study in the evening, review briefly in the morning, and protect your sleep.

Fix number six. Explain it like you are teaching a ten year old.

Pick a concept. Explain it out loud, in simple words, with no notes.

The moment you get stuck, you have found a gap in your understanding.

Go back, fill the gap, and try again.

Some people call this the Feynman technique, named after the physicist Richard Feynman.

You can explain to a friend, a pet, or an empty chair. Teaching is just retrieval with a purpose.

One more tip. Connect new facts to things you already know. Add a picture, or a little story.

Connected facts are easier to retrieve, because your brain has more paths leading to them.
